In the humid climate of Southern Mississippi, dissolved sulfate does not just vanish; it migrates downward via a combination of water movement and unique subsoil chemistry.1. How Deep Does It Leach?In the highly weathered, coarse, sandy topsoils (the A horizon) typical of the Southern Mississippi Coastal Plain, sulfate acts as a highly mobile anion. Because the topsoil has a net negative charge, it repels the negatively charged sulfate ion. [1] (https://www.calciumproducts.com/sulfur-cycling-in-soils/)The Leaching Depth: Sulfate rapidly washes completely out of the top 15 to 30 cm (6 to 12 inches) of the soil profile.The Transition: It generally travels down past the active root zones of shallow-rooted plants and turfgrass, seeking the heavier, clay-rich subsoil strata usually encountered between 30 to 100+ cm (1 to 3+ feet) deep. [1] (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/233448817_Determination_of_Sulfate_Concentration_in_Soil_Depth_of_Sampling)2. Does It Accumulate in Deeper Strata?Yes, sulfate heavily accumulates in the deeper subsoil (the B horizon). While it behaves like nitrate in the topsoil, its behavior changes dramatically once it hits deeper clay layers due to two specific geochemical mechanisms: [1] (https://researchrepository.wvu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=3390&context=etd), [2] (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/233448817_Determination_of_Sulfate_Concentration_in_Soil_Depth_of_Sampling)Positively Charged Fe/Al Clays (Anion Exchange): The subsoils of Southern Mississippi are rich in iron and aluminium oxides (which give the regional subsoil its characteristic red and yellow hues). At the naturally acidic pH levels of these deeper strata, the surfaces of these iron and aluminium clays take on a positive charge. This creates a strong electrostatic attraction, allowing the subsoil clay to bind and trap the incoming dissolved sulfate. [1] (https://researchrepository.wvu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=3390&context=etd)Organic Matter Gradient: Topsoils are rich in organic matter, which blocks sulfate from binding. Deep subsoils have almost no organic matter, leaving the clay binding sites wide open to capture and accumulate sulfate. [1] (https://researchrepository.wvu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=3390&context=etd)Deep Groundwater and Geological StrataIf sulfate manages to bypass the subsoil clay and reaches the shallow water table (less than 30–60 metres deep), it enters a highly stratified groundwater system. [1] (https://egrove.olemiss.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2013&context=etd), [2] (https://www.ngwa.org/docs/default-source/default-document-library/states/GWQ_Mississippi.pdf)In the regional aquifers of South Mississippi (like the shallow portions of the Mississippi embyament aquifer system), sulfate concentrations stay relatively low under normal conditions because anaerobic microbial zones actively reduce dissolved sulfate back into solid iron sulfide minerals like pyrite over long periods. [1] (https://www.usgs.gov/publications/localized-sulfate-reducing-zones-a-coastal-plain-aquifer), [2] (https://www.usgs.gov/publications/properties-and-chemical-constituents-ground-water-lower-wilcox-aquifer-mississippi)However, in much deeper, ancient confined strata—thousands of feet down—water chemistry transitions completely, and dissolved solids (including deep-lying sulfates from ancient marine deposits or evaporite dissolution) accumulate heavily into dense brines.
